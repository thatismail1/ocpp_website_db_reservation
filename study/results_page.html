Three Errors, Three Fixes

A revised study of hierarchical bi-level dispatch for multi-hub electric bus opportunity charging. The first version left 125 under-voltage bus-minutes and blamed the linearisation. It was wrong: there are three distinct error sources, only one of them is the linearisation, and each needs its own mechanism. With all three addressed the feeder holds exactly 0.9500 p.u. with zero violations while the fleet takes its full energy.

§ 2 — Diagnosis

The three error sources, separated

The first version of this study conflated three things under one number. Separating them is the substantive change, and it took a certificate, a correction loop and a Monte Carlo to do it.

Error 1 — the envelope was not sound

The dispatched power envelope came from a sensitivity walk that perturbs one hub while holding the others at their dispatch. Nothing certifies what happens when all four move at once. Pushing every hub to its own limit simultaneously drops the LinDistFlow voltage to 0.9339 p.u. — a 0.016 p.u. violation of an envelope that was supposed to guarantee security.

The fix is a certificate rather than a margin. Under LinDistFlow every network constraint is monotone in hub power: the voltage sensitivity matrix 2R and the subtree incidence are both non-negative, so the worst case over a box of hub powers is attained at a corner. Certifying the two corners therefore certifies the entire box, and the largest such box is the solution of a four-variable LP per interval. The result is exact — corner voltage 0.9500 — and costs 0.2 s for the whole day. The price of soundness is a 12 % tighter envelope: mean up-margin falls from 660 to 578 kW.

Error 2 — LinDistFlow is optimistic, and increasingly so with loading

Measured against the nonlinear power flow at the same operating point, LinDistFlow over-predicts voltage by a mean of +0.00085 p.u., a 95th percentile of +0.00256 and a maximum of +0.00423 at the lightly-loaded dispatch — but the error grows with feeder loading, because the dropped terms are quadratic in current. A band sized at one operating point is therefore not a safe de-rating at another, which is exactly what the results show: a fixed 0.955 p.u. band leaves the same 23 bus-minutes as no band at all.

Correcting the model works where padding it does not. After each rolling dispatch the upper level evaluates the true AC voltage at its own plan and re-solves with the additive correction ucorr = uAC(P) − uLinDistFlow(P). Two passes suffice — the residual falls 0.0061 → 0.0004 → 0.0001 p.u.2 — and each pass costs one power-flow solve, about 15 ms. Violations go to zero and delivered energy rises, because the corrected model no longer needs a blanket de-rating.

Error 3 — forecast error enters where no hub can reach it

This one produced the study's most useful negative result. Injecting forecast error into the hub MPC changes nothing at all: its envelope and its price are both stated on controllable power, so site PV and load error cannot move its decisions. Widening the security band changes nothing either. The exposure is entirely in the upper level, and it is dominated by feeder-wide load forecast error at buses no hub controls.

Neither the envelope nor the band can absorb that, because both act on quantities downstream of it. The margin has to go on the forecast itself: planning against load inflated by zσ makes the dispatched envelope secure for the upper tail of the load distribution rather than for its mean. At 5 % inflation, forecast-induced violations go from 30–98 bus-minutes to zero, and the cost is 0.8 MWh less energy delivered per day.

Three things fall out. PV does not fix voltage. Tripling the canopies cuts the day's energy bill by a quarter and moves under-voltage exposure barely at all, because PV peaks at midday and the feeder's problem is at 19:00. Coordination has a hosting limit. At 16 vehicles and at 70 % feeder loading the framework can no longer reach zero — 20 and 167 bus-minutes remain. Past that point the answer is reinforcement, not control, and being able to locate that boundary is arguably the framework's most useful output for a planner. At 70 % loading the uncoordinated case also overloads the transformer (5169 kVA against a 5 MVA rating) while the coordinated one stays at 4327.

§ 5 — Coordination

Convergence and cost

ADMM primal residual

Consensus between feeder and fleet blocks, kW over 384 coupling variables. Log scale.

Rebuilding both blocks as true convex QPs — exact quadratic LinDistFlow losses, exact voltage-deviation penalty, exact quadratic proximal — replaced a piecewise-linear surrogate that floored at 337 kW after 15 iterations. The residual now reaches 1.0 kW in 6 iterations, and a full coordination round takes 2.1 s against 97 s before. The rolling scheme re-dispatches 24 times a day at about 5 s per tick including the AC correction, against a 60-minute tick — three orders of magnitude of headroom.

Hub MPCs solve in 84 ms mean, 270 ms max over 731 solves per case, against a 1-minute discretisation with a 5-minute control interval. A control interval of 2 minutes reproduces the same day to within 0.002 % of cost, which is what justifies the coarser setting in the sweeps.

§ 6 — Honest accounting

What is filled, and what is not

Closed since the first version

  • True QP ADMM. Residual converges instead of flooring; 45× faster.
  • Sound envelope. Monotonicity-based corner certificate, verified independently by pushing all hubs to their limits at once.
  • Rolling upper level. The first version coordinated once per day; the formulation specifies a rolling window, and now the implementation does too.
  • Model error handled by correction, not padding.
  • Sensitivity sweeps over PV, fleet size and feeder loading, including the hosting-capacity boundary.
  • Forecast-error Monte Carlo against the upper level, plus the robust-margin remedy.
  • PV curtailment is now a decision variable in the upper level, so high-penetration cases are feasible.

Still open — and these matter for a submission

  • Novelty is unestablished. Nothing here surveys the literature. Bi-level DSO-fleet coordination is a populated field; whether the opportunity-charging framing is unclaimed is a judgement that requires reading, not computing. This is the single largest risk to publication and no amount of simulation addresses it.
  • No literature baseline. C0 and C1 are self-built strawmen. A reviewer will want a published method reimplemented.
  • Monte Carlo is under-sampled at 6 and 2 seeds.
  • One feeder. IEEE 33-bus only. A second, larger or unbalanced network (IEEE 123, or a utility feeder) would test whether the monotonicity certificate and the correction loop generalise. Real network data was not available here and inventing it would have been worse than leaving the gap.
  • The centralised reference is a relaxation evaluated on 15-minute averaged profiles, which understates peaks and losses; it is not a strict bound on the 1-minute closed loop and the two are not directly comparable. A true centralised MILP at matching resolution was not built.
  • The robust margin is a fixed inflation, not a chance constraint. Sizing it from the forecast error distribution, with a stated violation probability, is the principled version and is not implemented.
  • The transit scenario is synthetic. Network data and vehicle physics are real; the timetable, PV profile and site loads are constructed.
